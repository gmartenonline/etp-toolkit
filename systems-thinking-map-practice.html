Map It in Practice

The Map It framework gives you the analytical structure. This page covers how that analysis works in the field: how to surface a community's experience, how to build the diagram with them, and what to watch for when group dynamics complicate the process. The prompting questions and session structure below are specific to ETP mapping; they aren't generic facilitation templates.

A community mapping session produces a shared diagram showing how the community's ecological and social problems connect and reinforce each other, and where a change might find traction. Collective knowledge is essential, but building it together in the room creates shared understanding and anchors commitment to whatever comes next. People who helped draw the map will engage with it differently than people who were handed one.

1. Agree on the problem

Before gathering losses, get the group to a shared starting point. This takes ten minutes and prevents the session from fracturing into separate threads later.

Ask: "In one sentence, what is the main problem this community is facing?" Write responses. If they diverge, find the common thread and name it. Write it at the top of the paper as the agreed framing for the session.

2. Open with loss, not process

Don't explain the exercise upfront. Start with a concrete question about lived experience:

"Think back 20 or 30 years. What was different here: in the forest, the water, the fishing, the farming, the animals, the soil? What do you remember that you don't see anymore? What has become harder that used to be easier?"

Write responses on the paper as they come. Don't organize yet. Invite different perspectives: what fishing families lost may differ from what farming households lost; what elders remember may differ from what younger people have seen. Make sure all of it goes on the paper: ecological losses and social ones together.

If the group is quiet, try: "What did your parents have access to that you don't?"

Then push back in time further. Ask about changes that happened before the visible decline, in the decades leading up to it: new land management practices, shifts in ownership, industries or markets that arrived and changed how people used the land or water. External pressures (government policies, new technologies, outside economic forces) belong on this list as much as local ones. Some of the earliest shifts are where the story actually starts.

3. Surface the pressures

Once you have a list of losses, ask about causes. No cause is too large or too small:

"Why is there less [fish / forest / water / healthy soil] than there used to be? What changed?"

Keep collecting on the same paper without organizing. People typically name external causes first: new industries, government land decisions, outside markets. Acknowledge these as real. Then ask: "And within the community, is there anything happening here that's also contributing?"

4. Build the map

Using the diagramming approach from Map It, pick one item, usually a core resource loss, and draw it in the center. Then ask:

"What caused this to happen? What happened first, before this?"

Draw the earliest cause they identify. Then: "What happened next?" Draw the arrow forward. Repeat this, working chronologically toward the present. The diagram grows as the story proceeds, and that's intentional: participants should see the chain extending as the session goes on.

Arrows can lead in multiple directions from the same event. They can also loop back to something already in the chain, which is when the diagram starts showing why things keep getting worse.

No impact is too small to map. Ecological changes spread across both the ecosystem and the social system. When participants mention something that seems minor, add it.

Facilitator Tip

When a vicious cycle first becomes visible in the diagram, stop and name it explicitly. "So this loops back to here, which means the problem feeds itself. That's the pattern we're looking for." These reinforcing loops are often the most important places to break.

The session is working when someone in the room says "so it keeps getting worse because…" That's the feedback loop becoming visible.

If the group gets stuck: "Does this connect to anything else on the board?" or "Is there anything important that's missing from this picture?"

Once the diagram feels complete, ask participants to identify the earliest cause in the chain: the event or change that started the whole sequence. Circle it. This is the negative tipping point: the moment the system began moving in the wrong direction. Label it. You'll return to it in Reverse It.

Facilitator Tip

Three things are distinct on this diagram: the negative tipping point (where the system began to tip); the specific location in the chain where the human-nature connection broke; and the nodes where a positive intervention might work. They often overlap, but not always. Knowing which is which matters for Reverse It.

5. Test and validate

Before closing, make sure the complete diagram is on the paper. Then read it back:

"So what we're seeing is: [walk through the loop]. Does this capture what's happening here? What's wrong with this picture? Is this leaving out any very important losses or potential causes?"

Give people explicit permission to correct it. Then ask: "Is there anything on this map that the community has some ability to change, even partially?" Circle those nodes lightly. This is a positive note to end on: the group has found places where they might have some traction.

Close with a brief, open question: "Is there anything in this diagram that we could change?" Don't take it further here. Just let the answers sit. That's the question Reverse It is built to answer.

Facilitator Note

This question appears again at the start of Reverse It in Practice. That's intentional; it's a bridge, not a repetition. Ending Map It with it open plants the question before the group has a framework to answer it. Reverse It gives them that framework.

6. Close and distribute

Before people leave, name what the group accomplished: they've described their situation as a connected system rather than a list of separate problems, and that's what points toward where a change could matter.

The map should leave the room with participants. Photograph the original. If time allows, redraw a clean version before distributing, especially if the map got complicated. A clear layout, with vicious cycles and the tipping point visually legible, helps people keep thinking between sessions.

Watch For

Performed agreement is not real agreement. In a field project in Northern Thailand, an outside development organization recommended cauliflower to a group of farmers whose previous crop, ginger, had been planted at the same organization's suggestion. Ginger was rotting by the roadside; the price had collapsed and it cost more to take to market than it was worth. When asked who would plant cauliflower, most raised their hands and stated acreages. Privately, after the officials left, the farmers were blunt: "We just need to be polite. Are you crazy? We're not gonna plant cauliflower after the shitty advice they gave us."

Hand-raising and stated commitments in a meeting are not agreement. Communities perform participation when they don't trust the process, or when they've been burned before. Running a process that performs participation while seeking ratification is the most common failure mode in this work. If you already know what the answer should be, hold it back until the group has generated their own candidates.

Consensus and silence. Traditional and Indigenous decision-making in much of the Global South operates by consensus, not majority rule, regardless of what formal governance structures have been imposed. Majority-rule dynamics produce false positives: visible agreement with no underlying commitment. The quiet person is not a yes. When someone nods, ask what the nod means. When the room goes silent after a proposal, that silence is information.

In many communities, people will raise hands to be polite without intending to act. Don't read it as agreement. Pause, ask the quiet people directly: "What do you see from your experience?"

Not everyone will be equally enthusiastic. Some will say these outsiders have been here before and gave bad advice. Others will say this seems different. That split is normal and shouldn't be papered over in the session. Success breeds success; early skeptics often become advocates once they see results from the first willing participants.

Impatience for solutions. Acknowledge the urgency: "I know it feels slow to spend time mapping when things are urgent. What we're trying to avoid is acting fast in the wrong place."

A map that gets too complicated. Pull back: "If you had to identify just three or four things driving the decline, what would they be?" Some maps do get complex; that's not always a problem. But participants should be able to read it.

Blame dynamics in gathering losses. When a community surfaces its own role in ecological decline, the conversation can turn accusatory. Fishing families blame farming families; younger people blame elders. The facilitator's job during losses-gathering is to keep the focus on the sequence of events, not on assigning fault. If blame surfaces, redirect: "We're building a picture of what happened and when; we'll get to what can be done about it."

New to facilitating?

If you haven't run participatory sessions with communities before, a few things before you start:

Space and materials. Large paper is ideal, but work with what's available: a whiteboard, a wall with sticky notes, or a cleared patch of ground and sticks. Two marker colors help distinguish community actions from their impacts on the ecosystem, but one color works. Have the slide deck reviewed before the session, not open on your laptop during it.

Pacing. Losses-gathering (steps 1–3) carries emotional weight. Don't rush it; don't let it run so long that energy drains before mapping begins. Read the room on timing.

Capture. Photograph the map at the end of the session and print copies before the next one. A shared map that participants can hold and show to others does work that a map on the facilitator's laptop cannot.

Between sessions. Distribute the map. A clean redraw with vicious cycles and the tipping point visually legible helps people keep thinking before Reverse It.
