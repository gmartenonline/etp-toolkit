Every tipping point success story follows the same basic structure.

It begins with a trigger. A new fishing technology arrives, or a drought hits, or a policy changes. Whatever the cause, it sets off a chain reaction. Changes in the ecosystem shift what’s possible for the community. Changes in the community put more pressure on the ecosystem. Ecosystem health falls further. Both spiral downward together.

The second part begins with a decision to act. A targeted intervention interrupts the spiral and starts a new one, this time toward recovery. As the ecosystem rebounds, local livelihoods improve. Improvement motivates further action. The gains compound, and the approach spreads to neighboring communities.

The Web of Interconnected Elements

Systems thinking looks at ecological decline as a set of interconnected elements across the human-environment system, tied together by causal relationships that keep conditions improving or worsening. That web is what makes recognizing the scope of the problem one of the greatest challenges in addressing decline. Identifying losses large and small begins to uncover chains of cause and effect, and some of those chains are responsible for the vicious cycles spiraling a community into crisis. How elements spread across the human-environment system are connected is rarely immediately apparent: men leaving their failed crops in Rajasthan villages to find work in the cities is not obviously linked to abandoning maintenance of their traditional rainwater-harvesting ponds, until connections are uncovered between how the dams built to create the ponds channeled rainwater runoff into the aquifer, which provided irrigation water for farming. And, since the departed men were no longer available to help maintain dams, these impacts reinforced each other.

A clear goal of reviewing the history of the problem and assessing the current situation can help bring to light the interconnected elements perpetuating decline in the human-environment system. Those key elements are revealed by going back in time to see how conditions have worsened, what actions or events triggered a change in conditions, how losses for the ecosystem have brought about changes in the human social system, and where chains of cause-and-effect have been set in motion. Surveying the range of changes also helps identify the vicious cycles driving decline, and the turning point towards it. As you explore the scope of the problem, no loss or pressure is too small to consider, because even small impacts can create a vicious cycle driving decline.

Though there are many ways to piece together the scope of the problem, our case studies of successful restoration share similarities in how a clear vision was reached. Outside organizations or individuals often played key roles in stimulating a reckoning and openness to fresh approaches, but the interconnection of different parts of the problem spread across the human-environment system emerged from storytelling and discussion among community members. The experience and memories of a cross-section of community members provided the rich detail needed to recognize the range of losses and sequences of cause-and-effect, and the history of the problem. The memory of elders was an especially rich resource for piecing together how things had changed and for recalling long-held and sustainable resource management practices that had been abandoned, or the sequence of events that unfolded when new ones were introduced. They remember how both their communities and ecosystems operated when they were healthier. In our Rajasthan case study, if one elder had not shared his memories from childhood of the seasonal dam maintenance by the community, a key element of the problem and solution would have been missed.

Scoping Goals

The scoping goals below are guides to clarifying the structure of the problem. Each is illustrated by an example from our Apo Island case study, in which a fishing village in the Philippines restored its collapsing fishery and way of life with the positive tipping point levers of establishing a marine sanctuary and creating strict rules for their fishing grounds. But first they pieced together their story to understand why their village was falling apart.

A shift from a better past.

What were conditions like before the decline? This helps identify what the community is trying to recover and how things went wrong.

On Apo Island

The Memory: Elders remembered when the reefs were healthy and fish were abundant, and the fishing techniques they used.

A trigger or starting point.

Was there a specific event or change that set things in motion? A new technology introduced, a policy change, a shift in land use or ownership?

On Apo Island

The Trigger: The introduction of destructive fishing methods led to overfishing and damage to the coral reef ecosystem.

A sequence of connected problems.

Does one problem seem to have caused another? You're looking for chains: this led to that, which made this worse, which caused that.

On Apo Island — the sequence
Destructive fishing methods → Damaged reefs and overfishing → Fewer fish → Greater dependence on destructive methods → Even fewer fish → Fishing further away → Less incentive to protect local waters → More destructive methods → More reef damage → ↺ Even fewer fish

Thresholds or turning points.

Moments when things got noticeably worse, or faster. These often mark where the system crossed into a new state.

On Apo Island

First turning point: Declining numbers of fish made destructive fishing essential to catch any fish at all.

Second turning point: Fishermen could no longer make a living from island fishing grounds and had to travel farther from home.

Impacts on the social system.

For each environmental loss, what has it meant for people's lives, income, and work?

On Apo Island

The Crisis: Eventually, there was nowhere close by with adequate fish, and the community faced collapse.
